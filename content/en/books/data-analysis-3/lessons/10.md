# The Pie-Chart Controversy and Its Alternatives

> The pie chart is probably the first chart every beginner learns — and the one data-visualisation experts recommend least. This lesson explains why it is problematic and what to use instead.

## Pie charts can be drawn, but only in narrow situations

```python
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use('Agg')
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

plt.rcParams['font.sans-serif'] = ['WenQuanYi Micro Hei']
plt.rcParams['axes.unicode_minus'] = False

channels = ['Online Store', 'Own Stores', 'Distributors', 'Live Streaming']
share = [42, 28, 18, 12]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 7))
ax.pie(share, labels=channels, autopct='%1.1f%%', startangle=90)
ax.set_title('Revenue by Channel')
fig.savefig('pie_basic.png')
print('saved')
```

`autopct='%1.1f%%'` displays the percentages; `startangle=90` makes the first slice start at 90°. **A pie chart only fits a "the whole is 100%" scenario** — share, composition, proportion.

## Why experts discourage pie charts

**The human eye is bad at comparing angles.** This is the fundamental flaw of pie charts.

```python
# two slices, which is bigger?
a, b = 27, 32
```

Output: the slices for 27% and 32% **look almost the same size**, yet many people answer "which is bigger?" incorrectly. By contrast, the length difference between two bars is obvious at a glance.

**Experimental conclusion**: judgement error for **length** is around 5%, while error for **angle/area** is over 20%. Pie charts use angles, so they are innately imprecise.

## Failure scenario 1: too many categories

```python
cats = ['Online Store', 'Own Stores', 'Distributors', 'Live Streaming', 'Group Buy',
        'Affiliates', 'Resellers', 'Corporate', 'Overseas']
vals = [25, 20, 15, 12, 8, 7, 6, 5, 2]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 7))
ax.pie(vals, labels=cats, autopct='%1.1f%%')
fig.savefig('pie_many.png')
```

Output: the **slices are as thin as toothpicks** and the labels crowd together or fall outside the chart. Beyond 5–6 slices a pie chart is essentially unreadable.

## Failure scenario 2: a 1% difference drawn as nearly equal

```python
vals = [26, 27, 25, 22]      # four nearly identical numbers
ax.pie(vals, labels=['A','B','C','D'], autopct='%1.0f%%')
```

Output: the four slices **look almost the same size**, yet the numbers differ by 5 percentage points. This "appears equal" visual deception is the pie chart's most common trap.

## Alternative 1: the bar chart (the safest)

**Any pie chart can be replaced by a bar chart, and the result is usually better.**

```python
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
bars = ax.barh(channels, share, color='#377eb8')
ax.set_xlabel('Share (%)')
ax.set_title('Revenue by Channel')

# label each bar with its value
for bar, v in zip(bars, share):
    ax.text(v, bar.get_y() + bar.get_height()/2, f'{v}%',
            va='center', ha='left', fontsize=10)
fig.savefig('bar_alt.png')
```

The strength of a horizontal bar chart: **length differences are obvious** — 5% and 32% are instantly distinguishable; it handles 10 or more categories; and labels never get crowded.

## Failure scenario 3: not sorting by size

```python
ax.barh(channels, share)        # follows the original order, looks messy
```

Output: highs and lows are interleaved and it is impossible to see which is the leader. **Slices and bars alike should be sorted from largest to smallest:**

```python
order = np.argsort(share)              # sort ascending
ax.barh(np.array(channels)[order], np.array(share)[order])
```

Once sorted, "the leading channel is right at the top", and the information density jumps.

## Alternative 2: a stacked horizontal bar (100% stacked)

When you want to emphasise that "the whole is 100%" without using a pie chart:

```python
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3))
left = 0
colors = ['#e41a1c', '#377eb8', '#4daf4a', '#984ea3']
for cat, v, c in zip(channels, share, colors):
    ax.barh(['Share'], [v], left=left, label=cat, color=c)
    ax.text(left + v/2, 0, cat, ha='center', va='center', color='white', fontsize=10)
    left += v
ax.set_xlim(0, 100)
ax.set_xlabel('Share (%)')
ax.set_title('Revenue by Channel (100% stacked bar)')
ax.legend(loc='upper center', ncol=4, bbox_to_anchor=(0.5, 1.15))
fig.savefig('stacked100.png')
```

The 100% stacked bar keeps the "the whole is 100%" meaning while comparing through **length** rather than angle.

## When a pie chart is acceptable

A pie chart is not "absolutely forbidden"; it has strict preconditions:

- **At most 5 categories**
- **The intent is to emphasise "this is 100% of a whole"** (composition, share)
- **The categories differ clearly** (the largest is at least 2× the smallest)
- **The reader only needs a rough impression**, not a precise comparison

For weekly business reports, the opening slide of a deck, or an audience without a technical background, pie charts still work — **their strength is that they convey "this is a composition" at a glance**, which a pie chart expresses most directly. But whenever precise comparison matters, switch to bars without exception.

## The donut chart does not help either

```python
ax.pie(share, labels=channels, autopct='%1.1f%%', wedgeprops={'width': 0.5})
```

A donut is just a pie with the centre hollowed out; at heart it still compares angles, so **none of the flaws are fixed**. The only use for the empty centre is to place a total — if you actually use it for that, it has some value; otherwise it is pure decoration.

## Recap

- The fundamental flaw of pie charts: **the human eye is bad at comparing angles** (over 20% error, versus 5% for length).
- Beyond 5–6 slices a pie becomes unreadable; for many categories always switch to bars.
- When differences are small (26% vs 27%) a pie chart draws them as "nearly equal" — its most dangerous trap.
- Alternatives: the horizontal bar chart (safest) and the 100% stacked bar (preserving composition semantics).
- Sorting matters: pie slices and bar segments alike should run largest to smallest.
- The pie chart's one irreplaceable strength: **it communicates "this is 100% of a whole" most directly.**
