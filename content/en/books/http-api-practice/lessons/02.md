# 02 Tim Berners-Lee: One Person, One Computer, and Three Things He Had to Build Himself

> He couldn't find a job after graduation and scraped by writing printer drivers. In 1980 he spent six months at CERN, and before leaving wrote a small program to organize his own notes — it was called ENQUIRE. Nine years later, the World Wide Web grew out of that program's idea.

## The Child Who "Thought About Thinking" on Trains

Tim Berners-Lee was born in London on June 8, 1955.

Both his parents were mathematicians, and both were **among the very first computer programmers**. His father Conway Berners-Lee and his mother Mary Lee Woods both worked on the **Ferranti Mark 1** — the world's first commercially produced general-purpose computer, delivered in 1951.

So this wasn't a child who "got into computers later". He **grew up in a programmer's household**; that's what was talked about at home.

He recalls a childhood habit: on trains he would spend long stretches **staring out the window, "thinking about thinking"** — puzzling over how ideas get strung together inside a person's head.

That habit paid off later. Because the essence of the Web was never "transfer a file". It was **"write down the connections between ideas"** — exactly what that boy was chewing on in the train.

## Oxford: He Soldered a Computer Together

In 1973 he went to The Queen's College, Oxford, to read physics.

While there he did something that looked insane at the time: **he built his own computer**. Parts included:

- A circuit board pulled from an old television
- A Motorola 6800 processor
- Various soldered components

No kit, no manual. Entirely by hand.

There was a sequel: he got **punished at university for using his own computer to play pranks** — he and a friend used it to hack into other people's systems. The university's response: **ban him from using university computers**.

For the man who would invent the Web, that's a bit ironic.

## After Graduation: No Job

He graduated from Oxford in 1976.

And then — **couldn't find a job**.

It sounds absurd, but the computing industry was small then, and it was clear he worked on very low-level systems software. He later recalled sending out many résumés and hearing nothing.

So how did he eat?

**Writing printer drivers for people.**

Very concrete work: a company bought a printer, it wouldn't talk to the existing computer, and someone needed a small program to make the two communicate. He took jobs like that.

This is actually revealing — **what he kept doing was "making two things that can't talk to each other talk"**. A printer and a computer couldn't talk: he wrote a driver. Later, information and people couldn't connect: he wrote the Web.

## 1980: Six Months and ENQUIRE

In June 1980 Berners-Lee got work at CERN — **a six-month software consultancy contract**.

He was 24, working on data processing and control-system software.

At CERN he hit that same old problem again: **you can't remember who knows what**.

He was a consultant and would be gone in six months (he genuinely intended to leave when the contract ended), so he especially needed a way to record "who knows what". So he wrote a small program called **ENQUIRE**.

### Where the Name Came From

It came from a book: **"Enquire Within Upon Everything"**.

A Victorian household reference manual, a household name in Britain. It contained everything: how to write letters, how to remove stains, how to cook, how to handle minor ailments. The title literally means "ask inside about everything".

They had this book at home when he was a child. He felt the name captured exactly what he wanted: **a place you could ask anything**.

### What ENQUIRE Did

ENQUIRE was, in essence, **a personal notes database with hyperlinks**.

It showed a card on screen with text on it, and some words in the text were **highlighted**. Select a highlighted word and you jumped to another card.

One card might say "the data for this detector is Zhang's responsibility", with "Zhang" highlighted; click it and you land on Zhang's card, which has his contact details and the other things he's responsible for.

**That's a hyperlink.** Though nobody called it that yet.

### ENQUIRE Differed From the Web in One Key Way

This difference matters, and Berners-Lee himself returned to it repeatedly:

**ENQUIRE's links were bidirectional.**

What does that mean?

If you created a link on card A pointing to card B, then **a link back to A automatically appeared on card B**. The system maintained the fact "these two cards are related", visible from both ends.

**Links on the Web are one-way.**

You put a link on your page pointing to my page — **I have no idea**. Nothing pointing back to you appears on my page automatically. That's why we have the concept of a "backlink", why Google's PageRank exists (essentially counting how many people point at you one-way), and why we have link rot — the page you linked to moved away, and it won't tell you.

**Why change to one-way?**

Because bidirectional links require **both ends to be in the same system**, managed by one database. ENQUIRE was one program on one machine, so it could do it. But the Web is **documents on machines belonging to different organizations all over the world pointing at each other** — you can't force someone else's server to maintain a reverse link for you.

**One-way is the price of being distributed.**

Berners-Lee wrote explicitly in his autobiography that ENQUIRE's bidirectional links were lost in the later Web, and that he regretted it. The Semantic Web and Linked Data he later pushed for were, to some extent, an attempt to recover that layer of relationship information.

### The Six Months Ended

At the end of 1980 the contract expired and he left CERN.

ENQUIRE stayed there and was used by some people for a while, but it **never spread** — it depended on specific hardware, and nobody ported it to other machines.

The program failed. But the idea in it survived: **the connections between pieces of information should be written down explicitly, not kept in someone's head**.

## The Years Between

After CERN he went to a British company working on real-time control systems and graphics software.

Then in 1984 he saw a job posting at CERN. **A permanent position.**

He applied, and went back.

This time he stayed. And this time CERN's scale — and its problems — were far bigger than in 1980.

## 1989: The Proposal

On March 12, 1989, he submitted a document at CERN.

Title: **"Information Management: A Proposal"**.

### What the Proposal Said

The core wasn't "build a website". It was this line of thought:

> CERN has a huge amount of information scattered across different systems: personnel records, experiment data, equipment manuals, meeting notes, paper drafts. Different formats, different systems, mutually incompatible.
>
> Rather than building one giant unified database to stuff them all into (impossible at an organization with CERN's turnover), **build a thin linking layer and let existing things point at each other**.

The key insight:

**Don't unify storage. Unify reference.**

Unifying storage means moving all data into a new system — enormous cost, enormous resistance, and it goes stale. Unifying reference means every system stays as it is, and you only add a convention for "how to point at it".

This turned out right again and again. Today you write a web page pointing at someone else's resource and need no cooperation from them at all.

### The Diagram in the Proposal

The proposal included a diagram: a pile of documents with lines between them, forming a web.

He labelled this structure: **hypertext**.

He didn't invent the word.

## "HyperText" Is Much Older Than the Web

That's the question in the title — **why is it called HTTP? What on earth is HyperText?**

### 1945: Vannevar Bush's Memex

In 1945, just after World War II, **Vannevar Bush**, head of the US Office of Scientific Research and Development, published an article in *The Atlantic Monthly* called **"As We May Think"**.

Its central anxiety:

> Human knowledge is growing explosively, but our means of retrieving it are stuck at "alphabetical order" and "classification trees". **Indexes and classifications are linear and tree-shaped, but human association is web-shaped.**

He proposed an imaginary machine called the **Memex**:

A desk-sized machine holding books, newspapers, letters and photographs on microfilm. The user could search by keyboard and read on a viewer.

**But the crucial part wasn't storage — it was the "trail":**

> The user could **create an association** between any two items, name it, and **jump back and forth along it**. Trails could be chained into a "chain of association". Once someone had built their web of trails, they could **copy the whole thing to another person**.

That was 1945. No internet, no personal computers, storage on microfilm. **And it describes hypertext exactly.**

Bush's word was "associative trails", not hypertext, but the idea is identical.

### 1965: Ted Nelson Coins the Word

In 1965 a man named **Ted Nelson** presented a paper at an ACM conference using the word **"hypertext"** for the first time.

His definition:

> **Non-linear text** — text not organized head-to-tail, but letting the reader jump along links.

"Hyper" comes from Greek, meaning "beyond, above". So:

- **text** = linear writing, read start to finish
- **hypertext** = writing that transcends linearity, that you can jump through

Hence: **hyper + text**.

Ted Nelson went on to spend decades on Project Xanadu, an ambitious hypertext system aiming at bidirectional links, versioning, and royalty tracking. **It was never actually completed** — one of software history's most famous perpetual vaporware projects. But the word survived.

### So That's Where HTTP's Name Comes From

**HyperText Transfer Protocol**:

- **HyperText**: non-linear, jumpable documents
- **Transfer**: transport
- **Protocol**: protocol

Together: **a protocol for transferring hypertext**.

The name precisely marked its original purpose: **moving hypertext documents from a server to a browser**.

Which buried a slightly awkward problem: **it's called "HyperText Transfer Protocol", but hypertext is largely not what it transfers now.**

Today you send JSON over HTTP, and images, and video, and binary files. Almost none of it is hypertext.

**The name stayed; the use changed.** That's common in technical standards — a name records the moment of its birth, not what it became.

## "Vague, but exciting"

After the proposal went up, Berners-Lee's division head **Mike Sendall** wrote a comment by hand on the cover:

> **"Vague, but exciting."**

The line became legendary because it captured the situation so exactly.

**Why "vague"?**

Because the proposal genuinely didn't spell out implementation details. It described the vision of "documents linking to each other" without saying:

- How links are represented
- How addresses are written
- What protocol carries them
- How to make different systems all work

Those all had to be filled in later. The proposal even floated several possible technical routes without choosing.

**Why "exciting"?**

Because Sendall could see: if this could be built, it wouldn't solve "CERN's document management" — it would solve **information organization** in general.

The outcome: CERN **did not approve it as a formal project**, but did not forbid it either. Sendall gave him room — let him **try it with part of his time**.

That's common in research institutions: no budget, no headcount, but no obstruction either. **A lot of important things grow half-legally, just like that.**

## 1990: The Formal Proposal and the NeXT

The 1989 proposal didn't become a project immediately. Berners-Lee kept waiting.

By 1990 he wrote a more formal proposal with his colleague **Robert Cailliau** (Belgian, an engineer at CERN).

On November 12, 1990, the new proposal was submitted. This one was more concrete and listed what would be built.

It was approved. CERN gave him a **NeXT computer**.

### Why the NeXT Mattered

NeXT was made by NeXT, the company Jobs founded after being pushed out of Apple. It wasn't a commercial success, but it was superb for developers:

- Unix-based (Mach kernel + BSD)
- Objective-C, with strong object-oriented support
- A visual interface tool called **Interface Builder** — you dragged controls to draw your interface
- A complete toolchain included

**Interface Builder was the key.** Berners-Lee said many times that being able to build the first browser so quickly owed a lot to the NeXT environment — drag, drop, the window and buttons exist, and he could spend his energy on protocol design instead of interface code.

### One Person, Three Parts

In the second half of 1990 he started writing.

He had to build three things at once (the three-part package from lesson 01), and they depended on each other:

**1. URL (Uniform Resource Locator)**

For document A to point at document B, there must be a globally understood way to name B.

The format he designed was roughly:

```
scheme://host/path
http://info.cern.ch/hypertext/WWW/TheProject.html
```

**Notice the `//`.** He later said something delightful in public:

> If I'd known, I'd have dropped those two slashes.

They serve no syntactic purpose — they just followed an existing writing convention (the Apollo domain system's path notation), and after that nobody dared remove them. Every URL in the world still carries the two slashes he regrets.

**A good lesson: the hardest things to change in a protocol are the syntax details you didn't think through at the start.** Once the whole world writes to it, it never changes.

**2. HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol)**

With addresses, the two machines must agree how to fetch things back.

He designed a protocol that was **extremely simple** (lesson 03 shows what it actually looks like):

- The client sends one line: `GET /path HTTP/1.0`
- The server replies with a status code + headers + body
- Then the connection closes

How simple? **Simple enough that you can type it by hand with `telnet`**, no special software needed. Lesson 03 will have you actually do that.

**3. HTML (HyperText Markup Language)**

What comes back must render on screen, and **must be able to embed links**.

He borrowed the angle-bracket tag idea from SGML (Standard Generalized Markup Language), defining a small set of tags including the crucial one:

```html
<a href="http://example.com/other.html">click here</a>
```

`a` is **anchor**, `href` is **hypertext reference**. What goes inside `href` is a URL.

**These three interlock**: `href` holds a URL, the URL says `http://`, and HTTP carries the HTML. Miss any one and nothing turns.

## December 1990: First Website, First Browser

By December 1990 he had:

- **The world's first web server** (on that NeXT)
- **The world's first web browser** (also on that NeXT)
- **The world's first website**: `info.cern.ch`

That NeXT is still kept at CERN, with a handwritten note taped to it:

> **"This machine is a server. DO NOT POWER IT DOWN!!"**

Someone photographed that note, and it became one of the internet's most famous images.

### The First Browser Could Edit

The first browser was called **WorldWideWeb** (same as the Web itself, since it was simply "the tool for viewing the Web"). To avoid confusion with the Web as a concept, it was later renamed **Nexus**.

One important feature: **it could edit, not just view**.

In Berners-Lee's original vision, web pages were **read-write**: you see a page, spot a problem, edit it and save. The Web was a collaborative authoring space, not a publishing platform.

The "write" half got cut. For practical reasons:

- Editing means handling **concurrency** (two people edit at once — then what?)
- Handling **permissions** (who may edit)
- Handling **versions** (how to roll back a bad edit)

None of that was achievable on the 1990 Web. So the Web became **read-only** for a long stretch.

It came back slowly: wikis (WikiWikiWeb, 1995), blogs, comments, collaborative docs. **The original read-write vision took over twenty years to be partially realized.**

## August 6, 1991: The Public Post

On August 6, 1991, he posted to the Usenet newsgroup `alt.hypertext`, roughly saying:

> The World Wide Web project aims to make all documents accessible to each other via hyperlinks… if you'd like to take part, come and get the code.

That day is usually taken as the **Web's first public appearance**.

Note where he posted: `alt.hypertext` — the **hypertext** newsgroup. Because at the time the Web was understood as "another hypertext system", in the same category as Ted Nelson's Xanadu and Apple's HyperCard.

Nobody realized its scale would be different.

## Why This History Helps You Write Code

### 1. "Don't unify storage. Unify reference."

This was the proposal's single most important line, and the reason it beat competing approaches.

Compare:

| Approach | What it does |
|---|---|
| Giant database | Move all information into one new system, unify the format |
| Hyperlink layer | Every system stays as-is; add a convention for "how to point" |

The first needs everyone to cooperate in reworking themselves. The second — **anyone can start using it unilaterally**.

That explains a basic fact of writing HTTP code: **you can point at any resource without the other side cooperating.** You `GET` any URL without registering anything on their server.

### 2. One-way Links Are the Price of Distribution

ENQUIRE had bidirectional links; the Web doesn't. Not because "nobody thought of it" — because **it's impossible in a distributed system**.

That price shows up in things you meet every day:

- **Dead links**: the page you linked moved, nobody tells you
- **Search engines**: Google has to crawl the whole web precisely because it must **infer** the reverse relationships nobody maintains
- **Citation tracking**: "who referenced me" is something HTTP itself cannot give you

Understand this and you'll think, when designing APIs: **if two of your resources reference each other, HTTP won't maintain consistency for you** — you must handle it.

### 3. The Hardest Thing to Change in a Protocol Is Syntax Detail

Those two slashes: Berners-Lee regretted them for thirty years and couldn't remove them.

Because once a protocol is deployed it **cannot be changed**. Every URL in the world contains them; every parser in the world recognizes them.

Practical meaning for you: **think the first version through for any external interface (API paths, data formats).** Later change costs not "edit some code" but "persuade everyone to change with you".

### 4. "Cool URIs don't change"

In 1998 Berners-Lee wrote a short piece titled **"Cool URIs don't Change"**.

The core claim:

> Your page address should **never change**. Don't change the URL because you changed tech stack, reorganized departments, or redesigned.

Why? Because **other people link to you**. Change your address and their links break, and they'll never know.

The advice in that piece still holds today:

- Don't put **technical details** in URLs (`index.php`, `.asp`) — switching languages forces a URL change
- Don't put **organizational structure** in URLs (`/dept/marketing/`) — reorganizing forces a change
- Don't put **things that change** (author name, date, version number)

**Only the stable identity of the content.**

This matters especially for API authors — your API paths *are* your URLs. Think before you fix them.

## Recap

- Tim Berners-Lee, born London 1955, **both parents among the earliest programmers** (Ferranti Mark 1)
- After Oxford he **couldn't find a job** and got by writing printer drivers
- He spent **6 months** at CERN in 1980 and wrote **ENQUIRE** — named after *Enquire Within Upon Everything*
- **ENQUIRE's links were bidirectional; the Web's are one-way.** One-way is the price of distribution, and he later said he regretted losing it
- **"hypertext" came from Ted Nelson (1965)**; the idea traces to **Vannevar Bush's Memex (1945)** and its "associative trails"
- The 1989-03-12 proposal: manager Mike Sendall wrote **"Vague, but exciting"** — no project, but room to try
- On 1990-11-12 the formal proposal with Robert Cailliau was approved and CERN supplied a **NeXT** (Interface Builder sped development enormously)
- December 1990: `info.cern.ch` plus the first browser (**originally WorldWideWeb, able to edit**, renamed Nexus; the read-write vision shelved for twenty-plus years)
- 1991-08-06: post to `alt.hypertext`, the Web's public debut
- **`//` is the syntax detail he regretted for thirty years and could never remove**
- **Cool URIs don't change** (1998) — keep technology, organization, and volatile info out of URLs

```quiz
type: function
q: A URL is made of "scheme://host/path". Write split_url(url) that splits on the string "://" and returns a tuple (scheme, host_and_path). If the url contains no "://", return (None, url) instead — don't raise. Careful: don't treat the slashes inside "://" as part of the path.
func: split_url
starter: |
  def split_url(url):
      # Hint: first check whether "://" is in url
      # If so, split on it: before is the scheme, after is host+path
      # If not, this isn't a complete URL: return it as-is
      return (None, url)
cases: |
  "http://info.cern.ch/a/b.html" -> ("http", "info.cern.ch/a/b.html")
  "https://example.com" -> ("https", "example.com")
  "just-a-path" -> (None, "just-a-path")
  "ftp://host/x" -> ("ftp", "host/x")
hint: Check with "://" in url; split using url.split("://", 1) — the second argument 1 means cut only once, otherwise another "://" in the path gets shredded
explain: This is the real consequence of "choose your delimiter well" in protocol design. "://" is a three-character combination, safer than a lone ":" because the port after the hostname also uses ":". And split's second argument 1 matters — it guarantees only the first cut, so a later "://" in the path isn't shredded
```
