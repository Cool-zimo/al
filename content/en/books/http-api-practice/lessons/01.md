# 01 Before HTTP: The Night Someone Tried to Type LOGIN and Crashed

> On the night of October 29, 1969, two students tried to send five letters from Los Angeles to San Francisco. They got two across, and then the system crashed. That was the first computer network transmission in history — and the story of HTTP has to start twenty years earlier, with that moment.

## A Failed Beginning

On the night of October 29, 1969, in a small room at UCLA, a student named Charley Kline sat at a terminal, about to send a message to Stanford Research Institute, 500 kilometers away.

He meant to type `LOGIN` — to log into the remote host.

He typed `L`. Asked whether they got it. They had. He typed `O`. They had that too. He typed the third letter, `G`, and **the system crashed**.

So the content of the very first computer network transmission in human history was **`LO`**.

An unfinished word. A crash.

But the line strung between those two machines grew into a net that now covers the planet. HTTP is just one thread in the topmost layer of that net — **to understand HTTP, you first have to know what it's standing on.**

## Why You'd Want a Network With No Center

To understand ARPANET's design, you have to understand the problem it was solving.

In the late 1950s, the US Department of Defense's Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA, later DARPA) faced a very practical anxiety: **if war broke out and the communication hubs were destroyed, would the entire country's communications collapse instantly?**

The telephone network of the day used **circuit switching**: you dialed, the exchange **built a dedicated physical line** between you and the other party, that line belonged to you alone for the duration, and was released when you hung up.

Blow up any node along that path and the call dies. Worse — **the exchange centers are natural central nodes**. Destroy a few hubs and national communication is finished.

In 1964, Paul Baran at RAND proposed a different idea, called **packet switching**:

**Don't send the message whole. Cut it into small pieces, and let each piece find its own way.**

Here's how it works:

1. You want to send a sentence — first **cut it into several small packets**
2. Each packet gets the **destination address** written on it, like a letter
3. Each packet is dropped into the network and **routes independently**
4. A node blows up along the way? Doesn't matter — **later packets automatically route around it**
5. Once all packets arrive, they're **reassembled into the original message**

What's the key difference?

**Circuit switching is "build a dedicated highway first, then drive on it". Packet switching is "take the car apart, ship the parts over existing roads, reassemble at the destination".**

The latter has no center and no single point of failure. Any road can break and packets will find another. This property was emphasized again and again: **the network was designed to survive a nuclear strike**.

Around the same time, Donald Davies at the UK's National Physical Laboratory independently came up with the same idea — and **coined the word "packet"**. The English word packet, in this sense, only dates from the 1960s.

## The Nodes Weren't "Switches" — They Were Computers

Packet switching involved one more design decision that reaches all the way to today's internet:

**The devices forwarding packets in the middle of the network aren't dumb operators. They're computers that think.**

In the telephone network, a switch just connects a line; it doesn't understand what you said. In ARPANET, each node was a small computer (called an IMP, Interface Message Processor) that would:

- Receive a packet
- **Read where it's going**
- Consult its own routing table and decide which neighbor to forward to
- Send it on

This design later evolved into an important principle called the **end-to-end principle**:

> The network only promises to **make its best effort to deliver the packet**. What the packet means, whether it needs retransmitting, whether the data is correct — **leave those clever bits to the computers at the two ends**.

This principle explains why HTTP looks the way it does: **the network layer is deliberately dumb, pushing all complexity up to the topmost application layer**. That's why when you write HTTP code today you have to handle timeouts, retries, and status codes yourself — **because the layers below deliberately don't**.

Once you get this, a lot of "why is HTTP so annoying" questions dissolve. It's not bad design; it's complexity deliberately handed to you by the layering.

## 1974: Letting All the Networks Talk to Each Other

ARPANET succeeded, but a new problem appeared fast: **other people were building networks too**.

Britain had the NPL network, France had CYCLADES, Hawaii had ALOHAnet (a wireless packet network), and later there were satellite networks and radio networks. Each used its own protocol and **none could talk to the others**.

It was as if every country had built railways, but with different gauges, so trains stopped dead at the border.

In 1974, Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn published a paper, *A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication*. What they proposed became known as **TCP/IP**, and the core idea was:

**Don't invent another new network. Build a "network of networks" (an internetwork) — wrap a common protocol around each network so different networks can communicate.**

The word Internet is a contraction of **inter**connected **net**works. The internet = a network of networks. From the very beginning it meant "connecting a bunch of networks that can't talk to each other".

Their design contained a crucial trade-off:

- **IP** (Internet Protocol) handles "get the packet to the right machine" — **it does not guarantee delivery, and does not guarantee order**
- **TCP** (Transmission Control Protocol) runs on top of IP and handles everything IP ignores — retransmit lost packets, reorder scrambled ones, slow down when congested

The end-to-end principle again: **the lower layer only delivers; reliability is added by the two ends**.

What those two built became the common language of the entire internet.

## January 1, 1983: A Switch-Over Day With No Going Back

On January 1, 1983, every host on ARPANET was required to **switch, on the same day**, from the old protocol NCP to TCP/IP.

This was radical engineering — no gradual rollout, no phased migration. A hard deadline. Later, this kind of "everyone switches at once" move became known in systems engineering as a **flag day**.

Machines still running the old protocol after that day were simply disconnected from the network.

**Why dare do that?** Because without a unified protocol there is no internet. Keeping two protocols running in parallel means the "network of networks" never gets built.

The success of that switch marks **the actual birth of the internet**.

## By Now There Was Email, FTP, and Newsgroups

By the mid-1980s, quite a few applications were running on the internet:

| Application | Appeared | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| Email | 1971 | Send mail (Ray Tomlinson — he picked `@` as the separator) |
| Telnet | 1970s | Log into a remote host |
| FTP | 1971 | Transfer files |
| Usenet | 1980 | Newsgroups, distributed discussion boards |

Notice what's missing from that table — **HTTP**.

Because it hadn't been invented yet.

To fetch a file back then, you had to know which machine and which directory it lived on, then connect with FTP and download it. **There was no such action as "click and jump there".**

Information existed, but as **isolated islands**:

- You had to know where things were first (someone told you, or you read a directory file)
- Change machine, change directory, and the path changed
- One document referenced another? You noted it yourself and went hunting manually

The worse problem was **knowledge lost through staff turnover**.

## Tim Berners-Lee's Actual Problem

In 1989, at CERN, there was a British software engineer named Tim Berners-Lee.

CERN was then the world's largest particle physics laboratory, with thousands of researchers from dozens of countries. Their work had these characteristics:

- **Extremely fast turnover**: PhD students arrive and leave within two or three years; visiting scholars stay a few months and go home
- **Extremely long project cycles**: an accelerator experiment can take ten years from design to result
- **Knowledge depends heavily on word of mouth**: Zhang knows where the calibration data for a certain device is, but once he leaves, Li can't find it

Berners-Lee later described the essence of the problem over and over: **the information isn't missing — the connections between pieces of information were never written down**.

A senior researcher carries a huge web in their head: this report cites that measurement, the fix for that breakdown is written in someone's notebook, this detector's parameters have to be read against another's. **But that web exists only in his head.** When he leaves, the web breaks.

The retrieval systems of the day (including CERN's internal one) all required you to **search by some classification**: by project, by author, by date. But none of them solved the fundamental problem — **information isn't a tree, it's a web**.

Force web-shaped information into a tree-shaped classification, and the part that doesn't fit gets lost.

## March 1989: The Proposal

On March 12, 1989, Berners-Lee submitted a document to CERN titled:

**"Information Management: A Proposal"**

What he described was not "a better search engine". It was **a mechanism for letting any two documents link to each other**.

The core idea:

> Rather than making people remember "where things are", **let the document itself point at what it references**.

You write a report, it mentions another measurement, so you mark that sentence and make it point directly at that measurement. The reader clicks and jumps — **without needing to know which machine or directory that data lives on**.

That's **hypertext**.

"Hyper" means "beyond". Ordinary text is linear — read from start to finish. Hypertext is **web-shaped** — you can jump from any point to any other.

Berners-Lee's boss, Mike Sendall, wrote a line on the cover of that proposal that became legendary:

> **"Vague, but exciting."**

That line is perfect, because it captured the proposal exactly: **the idea was fuzzy, but the potential was visible**. CERN gave him time and a NeXT computer to try.

## Three Parts, None Optional

Berners-Lee's insight was that having the *idea* of links wasn't enough. Three things had to be built at once.

**First, a uniform way to address things: URL**

For document A to point at document B, you first need a globally unique way to name "which one is B".

`https://example.com/a/b.html` is a URL (Uniform Resource Locator). It encodes "which machine" and "which thing on that machine" into one string, usable worldwide.

**Second, a transfer protocol: HTTP**

With addresses, the two machines still need to agree on "how to fetch it back". That's HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) — it defines what the client says and what the server replies.

**Third, a language to describe documents: HTML**

What comes back has to be renderable, and **able to embed links**. That's HTML (HyperText Markup Language).

These three — **URL (where), HTTP (how to fetch), HTML (what it looks like)** — were all designed by Berners-Lee (with help from colleagues) around 1990.

**They're one package; missing any one, nothing works.** URL without HTTP: you know where it is but can't fetch it. HTTP without HTML: you get back plain text you can't click.

## Late 1990: The World's First Website

Around December 1990, Berners-Lee set up the world's first website on a NeXT computer.

That machine is still at CERN, with a handwritten note taped to it:

> **"This machine is a server. DO NOT POWER IT DOWN!!"**

The world's first website was at `info.cern.ch` — under the CERN domain, containing an explanation of what the World Wide Web was and how to use it.

He also wrote the world's first web browser. It was originally called **WorldWideWeb** (same name as the web itself), later renamed **Nexus** to avoid confusion.

And that browser **could edit, not just view** — in Berners-Lee's original vision, web pages were read-write; everyone could edit what they saw. The editing half was later dropped, and for a long stretch the Web became read-only.

On August 6, 1991, he posted to the `alt.hypertext` newsgroup on Usenet, introducing the World Wide Web to the public. That date is usually taken as the **Web's first public appearance**.

## 1993: CERN Gave It to the World

This is the most important decision in the whole story.

On April 30, 1993, CERN announced that **World Wide Web technology was entering the public domain — free for anyone to use, no patent fees.**

CERN didn't sell it, didn't patent it, didn't charge licensing fees.

What came of that?

Compare it with other routes of the era: there were commercial online services (CompuServe, AOL, France's Minitel) that were quite good, but they were **walled gardens** — content visible only inside their own system, not interoperable, and paid.

Opening the Web for free meant:

- Anyone could write a browser
- Anyone could run a server
- Anyone could make a website without asking permission

So it exploded.

**If CERN had chosen paid licensing in 1993, what you'd be using today would probably be some company's proprietary network, not this open Web.** This is often cited as the classic case of open standards beating closed systems.

## Why This History Helps You Write Code

You may be thinking: I just want to send a request from Python. Why do I need to know about two students in 1969?

Because HTTP carries a lot of design that looks **weird** — and **only makes sense once you know where it came from**:

**Why is HTTP stateless?**
Because Berners-Lee's problem was "documents linking to each other", not "a user logging in to shop". Fetch one document, disconnect. State was bolted on later (that's Cookie, lesson 10).

**Why are paths written hierarchically, like `/a/b/c`?**
Because he needed a **uniform namespace** so resource addresses look the same worldwide. Hierarchy is the simplest structure that scales.

**Why is the protocol layered?**
Because TCP/IP's end-to-end principle decided the **lower layer is deliberately dumb**, pushing complexity to the application layer. So you handle timeouts, retries, caching yourself — **that's not a defect; it's the flexibility the layering buys**.

**Why did HTTP need new versions later (lessons 4 and 5)?**
Because it was designed for a world of "occasionally fetching a document". Then it was made to carry video, real-time chat, bank transactions — **every one of its original assumptions became obsolete**.

So every "rule" you learn next has a reason behind it. **Know the reason and you'll remember it — and know when you can break it.**

## Recap

- On October 29, 1969, the first network transmission got only **`LO`** across before crashing
- **Packet switching** (Paul Baran / Donald Davies) cuts messages into packets that route independently, with no central node — so the network survives attack
- **End-to-end principle**: the network only makes a best effort; the clever parts belong at the ends. This is why you handle timeouts and retries yourself
- In 1974 Cerf and Kahn proposed TCP/IP; **"Internet" literally means a network of networks**
- On January 1, 1983, ARPANET switched entirely to TCP/IP on one day (a flag day)
- In March 1989, Tim Berners-Lee submitted his proposal; his boss wrote **"Vague, but exciting"**
- The first website `info.cern.ch` arrived in late 1990, and in 1993 **CERN gave the Web away for free** — the key to beating the commercial networks of the day
- HTTP is one of three: **URL (where) + HTTP (how to fetch) + HTML (what it looks like)** — none is optional
